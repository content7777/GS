📜 History of Banking in India

Origins of banking and how RBI evolved from private banks

Earliest Banks

Banking in India traces back to a few early, less significant setups before the major ones:

  • 1680 — A bank set up in Madras
  • 1720 — A bank set up in Bombay (Bombay Bank)

These were not very significant. The first truly significant bank was:

Bank of Hindustan — 1770, considered the first significant bank in India.

The Presidency Banks

Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras were important centers because they were centers of British power. Calcutta was the most important (capital of Bengal), so the first major bank was set up there.

1806 — Imperial Bank of Calcutta (Bank of Calcutta)
1840 — Imperial Bank of Bombay
1843 — Imperial Bank of Madras
1921 — These three merged to form the Imperial Bank of India
1955 — Renamed to State Bank of India (SBI)
SBI is currently India's oldest operational bank, with roots traceable back to 1806.

🏛️ Reserve Bank of India (RBI)

India's Central Bank — history, structure & role

Formation History

  • Originally started as an independent body — owned by its shareholders.
  • Hilton Young Commission (1926) recommended establishing a central bank to regulate other banks.
  • RBI Act, 1934 was passed based on this recommendation.
  • RBI began its actual working from 1st April 1935.
DetailInfo
Original HeadquartersKolkata
Current HeadquartersMumbai (from 1937)
First RBI GovernorOsborne Smith (British)
Second RBI GovernorJames Braid Taylor
First Indian GovernorC.D. Deshmukh
Governor from 1949 mentionedSir Benegal Rama Rau (referred as "Subbarao" in transcript)

Nationalization of RBI

RBI was nationalized on 1st January 1949. This meant the Central Government took over full share-holding (ownership) from private shareholders by paying them for their shares — hence full control moved to the government.

Why is RBI called the "Central Bank"?

Because RBI regulates all other banks in the country. This power comes from the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, which gives RBI authority to:

  • Issue banking licenses
  • Set CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio) and SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) — banks must maintain a certain amount of deposits as reserves
Because RBI regulates all other banks, it is known as the "Bank of Banks."

Functions of RBI

Currency & Regulation

  • Regulates banks
  • Prints/issues all currency notes except the ₹1 note
  • The ₹1 note is issued by the Ministry of Finance (Government), not RBI
  • Notes are signed by the RBI Governor with the promise: "I promise to pay the bearer this sum" — this makes them Legal Tender

Monetary Policy

  • Formulates Monetary Policy (tools: CRR, SLR)
  • Acts as "Lender of Last Resort" — banks in financial trouble can approach RBI for funds when no one else lends to them

Types of Money

TermMeaning
Fiat MoneyLegal money / legal tender backed by the government (e.g., by RBI). Cannot be refused as payment.

🗂️ Classification of Banks

Based on the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934

Scheduled vs Non-Scheduled Banks

The Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934 classifies banks into:

  • Scheduled Banks
  • Non-Scheduled Banks

Commercial vs Cooperative Banks

Scheduled banks are further classified based on their motive:

Commercial Banks

Main motive is profit.

Cooperative Banks

Depositors themselves are the owners; motive is more social development than profit.

Public Sector vs Private Sector Banks

Commercial banks are further classified into:

TypeOwnershipExamples
Public Sector BanksGovernment ownedSBI, PNB
Private Sector BanksPrivately ownedHDFC, Kotak Bank
Foreign (Private) BanksPrivately owned, foreignCiti Group, HSBC, Deutsche Bank

Nationalization of Banks

1969 — 14 banks nationalized under Indira Gandhi's government
1980 — Second round of nationalization

Post amalgamation over time, there are currently 12 nationalized banks where the Central Government holds share ownership.

🌾 Regional Rural Banks (RRB)

ग्रामीण बैंक — serving rural India

Formation

  • First RRB set up on 2nd October 1975 in Moradabad, UP
  • RRB Act, 1976 gave RRBs statutory recognition
  • Recommended by the Narasimham Committee

Shareholding Pattern

StakeholderShare
Central Government50%
State Government15%
Sponsor Bank35%

A Sponsor Bank (e.g., SBI, PNB) is assigned to operate/run the RRB since they are professionals in banking, whereas Central/State Governments are not banking experts. Sponsor banks also get shareholding as an incentive to manage it well.

🏭 SIDBI & MSME

Small Industries Development Bank of India

SIDBI

SIDBI = Small Industrial Development Bank of India

  • Established: 2nd April 1990
  • Headquarters: Lucknow
  • Main role: Development of MSMEs (Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises)

MSME Classification Criteria

MSME = Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises

CategoryInvestmentTurnover

🏢 Other Financial Institutions

IRDAI, SEBI & National Housing Bank

IRDAI

IRDAI = Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India

  • Established through the IRDA Act, 1999 as a statutory body
  • Actually formed/functional from 2000

SEBI

SEBI = Securities and Exchange Board of India

  • Main function: Protect the interests of investors (in shares, mutual funds, etc.) and curb fraud
  • Actually established in 1988, but got statutory body status via an Act in 1992
  • Headquarters: Mumbai
  • Current Chairperson: Madhabi Puri Buch — first woman chairperson and first non-IAS chairperson

National Housing Bank (NHB)

  • Established in 1988
  • Set up through an Act of 1987

🌱 NABARD

National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development

Formation

  • Committee formed in 1979
  • Recommended by the B. Sivaraman Committee
  • Established on 12th July 1982

Role of NABARD

NABARD does not deal directly with the public. Unlike RRBs (which deal directly with people), NABARD supervises RRBs.
FunctionPerformed By
Regulates RRBsRBI
Supervises RRBs (lending practices, etc.)NABARD
Direct dealing with people (loans)RRB

NABARD provides financing through schemes like the Kisan Credit Card / Rupay Kisan Card, but this financing is channeled through RRBs to reach people — not given directly.

Main focus: financing agriculture and rural development.

💰 Microfinance Institutions (MFI)

Financial services for low-income populations

What is Microfinance?

MFIs offer financial services to low-income populations — households with annual income less than ₹3 lakh are eligible for micro loans (this limit was earlier ₹1.25 lakh, revised to ₹3 lakh; some are proposing revision to ₹5 lakh).

Includes: micro loans, micro savings, micro insurance.

Origins

  • Concept originated in Bangladesh in 1970
  • Mohammad Yunus — considered the "Father of Microfinance," founded the first MFI: Grameen Bank, Bangladesh (won the Nobel Prize for this)
  • First MFI in India: SEVA (Self Employed Women's Association) — formed in Bombay

PM MUDRA Scheme

MUDRA = Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency

Provides micro loans, categorized as:

CategoryLoan Amount
ShishuUp to ₹50,000
Kishor₹50,000 – ₹5 lakh
Tarun₹5 lakh – ₹10 lakh
These are collateral-free loans — no security/pledge needed to take these loans.

SHG vs JLG — Business Models

Self Help Group (SHG)

  • 10–20 people per group
  • Informal group structure
  • Usually people Below Poverty Line (BPL)
  • Common in rural areas
  • Goal: improve living conditions (e.g., Lijjat Papad)

Joint Liability Group (JLG)

  • 4–10 people per group
  • Small business venture, for-profit
  • Common in semi-urban and urban areas
⚠️ Exam tip: JLG group size (4–10 people) was asked in CGL 2022.

🏢 NBFC & NBFC-MFI

Non-Banking Financial Companies

What is an NBFC?

NBFC = Non-Banking Financial Company — a financial company that does not have a banking license.

  • Registered under the Companies Act, 1956 or Companies Act, 2013
  • Examples: Bajaj Finance, Muthoot Finance

Key Differences: NBFC vs Bank

FeatureBankNBFC
Demand Deposits✅ Accepted❌ Not accepted
Time/Term Deposits✅ Accepted✅ Accepted
Deposit Insurance✅ Insured (up to ₹5 lakh)❌ Not insured/guaranteed
Regulated byRBIRBI
CRR / SLR requirement✅ Mandatory❌ Not required
💡 Deposit insurance covers a maximum of ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank — regardless of how much was actually deposited (e.g., even if ₹50 crore was deposited, max payout is still ₹5 lakh).

NBFC-MFI

  • Recommended by the Malegam Committee (2010)
  • Criteria: NBFC must have given out at least 75% of its total assets as microfinance loans to be classified as NBFC-MFI

Note: For a bank, deposits it receives are its "liability," while loans it gives out are its "assets."
